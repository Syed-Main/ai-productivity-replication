\begin{abstract}
\noindent A central question in modern macroeconomics is whether enterprise adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) generates genuine total factor productivity (TFP) gains or merely reflects capital deepening and workforce reallocation. This study develops a two-stage empirical identification framework that formally disentangles AI-induced efficiency improvements from conventional capital and labour contributions across 166 countries and three major economic sectors between 2000 and 2024. In the first stage, we estimate structural sectoral production functions using instrumental variables in first differences (Anderson--Hsiao) and System GMM (Blundell--Bond), addressing Marschak--Andrews transmission bias and employing econometrician-standard capital stocks from Penn World Table 10.01 allocated via sectoral investment and value-added composition. In the second stage, we regress growth-accounted residual TFP growth on enterprise AI adoption rates from the OECD SDMX database. To resolve endogenous technology adoption and reverse causality, we construct a Bartik-style shift-share instrumental variable that interacts pre-existing 2010 fixed broadband infrastructure with global leave-one-out AI diffusion trends, evaluated alongside Limited Information Maximum Likelihood (LIML) and distributed-lag models. Our empirical findings indicate that contemporaneous enterprise AI adoption yields a statistically negligible direct impact on aggregate TFP ($\hat{\theta} = +0.015$, $p = 0.913$; IV $\hat{\theta} = -0.034$, $p = 0.944$), consistent with modern productivity paradoxes and the initial transition frictions hypothesized by the productivity J-curve. Subsample and heterogeneity analyses reveal that positive productivity returns emerge primarily in digitally mature OECD economies and industrial manufacturing, whereas developing economies experience substantial implementation frictions. A comprehensive battery of robustness tests---including translog production specifications, placebo historical falsification tests, and leave-one-out jackknife estimations---confirms the stability of our findings.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =====================================================================

The rapid diffusion of artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning, and algorithmic automation across enterprise workflows has re-ignited intense debate regarding the macroeconomic trajectory of modern productivity. Proponents argue that generative and analytical AI represents the quintessential general purpose technology (GPT) of the twenty-first century, poised to break decades of secular stagnation by automating cognitive routines, accelerating scientific discovery, and shifting the aggregate production frontier outward \citep{brynjolfsson2017, ager2023, babina2024}. Conversely, sceptics emphasize that despite pervasive adoption headlines, aggregate total factor productivity (TFP) growth across advanced and emerging economies remains historically subdued, echoing Robert Solow's famous 1987 aphorism that computers could be seen everywhere except in the productivity statistics \citep{gordon2016, acemoglu2024}.

Resolving this dispute empirically at the macroeconomic level presents severe econometric and conceptual difficulties. An observed expansion in gross output coinciding with the installation of enterprise AI systems cannot legitimately be attributed to an AI-driven productivity shock. Output growth may mechanically stem from rapid capital deepening (expenditures on specialized graphics processing units, server racks, cloud computing architecture, and data storage), parallel expansions in skilled labour inputs, or unobserved demand and macroeconomic cycles. Furthermore, national income and product accounts (NIPA) record hardware and software investments as tangible and intangible gross fixed capital formation, while organizational redesign, bespoke algorithmic fine-tuning, workflow adjustments, and internal data curation are rarely capitalized, obscuring the boundary between input accumulation and technological efficiency \citep{corrado2021}. 

This measurement challenge is compounded by two fundamental endogeneity problems. First, in estimating the underlying production elasticities of capital and labour, firms adjust variable inputs in response to productivity shocks observed by managers but unobserved by econometricians---the classical transmission bias identified by \citet{marschak1944}. Second, at the secondary stage of estimating the returns to technological adoption, AI adoption itself is highly endogenous: high-performing sectors and frontier economies with superior institutional capacity, higher human capital, and stronger initial digital infrastructure adopt AI faster, generating severe confounding and reverse causality. Naive Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regressions of productivity on AI metrics inevitably yield biased estimates.

This paper develops a rigorous, two-stage econometric framework designed to identify the causal productivity contribution of artificial intelligence while cleanly separating it from capital and labour deepening. Our methodology departs from existing cross-country productivity studies in five critical dimensions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Harmonized Multi-Source Panel Architecture:} We synthesize a panel covering 166 countries, three major economic sectors (Agriculture, Industry, and Services), and 25 years (2000--2024) by linking the World Bank World Development Indicators, the OECD SDMX enterprise ICT database, and the Penn World Table (PWT 10.01).
    \item \textbf{Econometrician-Standard Capital Stock Construction:} Rather than allocating crude perpetual-inventory capital stocks across sectors in proportion to employment shares---which mathematically forces capital-to-labour ratios to be identical across sectors---we benchmark national capital stocks to PWT 10.01 constant-price series (\texttt{rnna}) extended via World Bank GFCF, and allocate capital according to sectoral investment and value-added composition.
    \item \textbf{Structural Production Function Estimation:} We discard naive cross-sectional OLS in favor of panel instrumental variables in first differences (IV-FD, Anderson--Hsiao) and two-step System GMM \citep{arellano1991, blundell1998}, purging entity-specific unobservables and instrumenting input changes with lagged levels to resolve simultaneity and transmission bias.
    \item \textbf{Causal Identification via Bartik Shift-Share Instruments:} To identify the causal elasticity of AI adoption on growth-accounted residual TFP growth, we construct a Bartik-style shift-share instrumental variable that interacts predetermined pre-2010 fixed broadband infrastructure penetration with leave-one-out global enterprise AI diffusion trajectories.
    \item \textbf{Testing the Productivity J-Curve and Distributional Heterogeneity:} We explicitly estimate distributed-lag specifications spanning multiple horizons ($k \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$) to test the \citet{brynjolfsson2021} hypothesis that GPTs initially suppress measured productivity before organizational capital accumulates, alongside decompositions across income groups and sectoral classifications.
\end{enumerate}

Previewing our primary empirical results, we find that the structural capital elasticity across countries and sectors is substantial ($\hat{\alpha} \approx 0.31$ to $0.60$), while constant returns to scale is strongly rejected. When Solow residuals are purified of input accumulation, contemporaneous enterprise AI adoption exerts an economically modest and statistically insignificant direct effect on aggregate TFP ($\hat{\theta}_{OLS} = +0.015$, $p = 0.913$; Bartik IV-2SLS $\hat{\theta}_{IV} = -0.034$, $p = 0.944$). However, distributed lag and heterogeneity analyses show marked divergence: in OECD economies and high-tech industrial manufacturing, positive productivity returns begin to emerge over multi-year horizons, while emerging economies experience transitional adjustment costs. Placebo tests (randomizing pre-AI adoption), translog production estimations, and leave-one-out jackknife evaluations verify the robustness of these conclusions.

Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses policy implications and reconciles our findings with recent macroeconomic models, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Literature}
\label{sec:lit}
% =====================================================================

Our research intersects four active strands of the economic literature: growth accounting and general purpose technologies, microeconomic evidence on artificial intelligence, macroeconomic growth projections, and the econometrics of panel production functions.

\subsection{General Purpose Technologies and Growth Accounting}
The foundational architecture for measuring productivity originates from \citet{solow1957} and was operationalized into comprehensive multi-factor accounting by \citet{jorgenson1967}. In this neoclassical growth accounting paradigm, output growth is decomposed into input growth weighted by marginal factor shares, with the unexplained residual interpreted as costless shifts in the aggregate production frontier (total factor productivity). 

When applied to major technological transitions, however, standard growth accounting often encounters the paradox identified by \citet{bresnahan1995}: general purpose technologies (such as the steam engine, electrification, and digital computing) generate profound economic transformations that fail to appear in concurrent productivity statistics. As \citet{stiroh2002} and \citet{jorgenson2008} demonstrated in the context of the 1990s Information and Communication Technology (ICT) revolution, measured productivity accelerations materialized only after sustained capital deepening in computing hardware was paired with sweeping organizational and complementary structural adjustments. \citet{gordon2016} cautions, however, that digital innovations may exhibit diminishing marginal economic returns compared to the transformative Great Inventions of the second industrial revolution.

\subsection{Microeconomic Evidence on Artificial Intelligence}
In contrast to macroeconomic aggregates, recent microeconomic and firm-level research documents tangible benefits from AI adoption. Using administrative and job-posting data, \citet{babina2024} show that AI-investing firms experience faster growth in sales, employment, and market valuation, driven primarily by product innovation rather than cost displacement. Similarly, \citet{brynjolfsson2023} conduct a randomized control study of generative AI customer support assistants, finding a 14 percent average boost in issue resolution per hour, with the largest gains accruing to novice and lower-skilled workers. \citet{alderucci2020} document that patenting in AI technologies is associated with substantial firm-level revenue and employment premiums. 

Nevertheless, microeconomic productivity premiums do not automatically aggregate into macroeconomic expansions. As \citet{acemoglu2018, acemoglu2020} emphasize in their task-based framework, if technological adoption is characterized by ``so-so automation''---substituting capital for labour in routine tasks with only marginal cost reductions without creating new high-productivity tasks---aggregate economic growth remains tepid while labour share declines.

\subsection{Macroeconomic Assessments and the Productivity J-Curve}
At the aggregate level, \citet{acemoglu2024} develops a formal macroeconomic evaluation of AI exposure, calculating that fewer than 5 percent of economic tasks can be cost-effectively automated by computer vision and large language models over a ten-year horizon, translating into a modest cumulative TFP increase of no more than 0.55 percent (or less than 0.06 percent annually). In complementary work, \citet{filippucci2024} evaluate OECD cross-country exposure and find substantial heterogeneity based on industrial structure and technological readiness.

The primary explanation for delayed macroeconomic impacts is the \emph{Productivity J-Curve} formalized by \citet{brynjolfsson2021}. They demonstrate that when a new GPT emerges, firms must redirect substantial capital and labour toward creating intangible assets---reorganizing management structures, retraining personnel, accumulating proprietary datasets, and rewiring business workflows. Because national accounts count this diverted effort as input expenditures while omitting the resulting unmeasured intangible capital from output, measured productivity growth initially falls or stagnates. Only in subsequent periods, when intangible capital begins delivering productive services without further adjustment costs, does measured TFP accelerate.

\subsection{Identification in Panel Production Functions}
Estimating production functions is plagued by endogeneity. Since \citet{marschak1944}, econometricians have recognized that profit-maximizing firms adjust input demands in response to anticipated productivity shocks, creating positive correlation between regressors and the error term. While structural proxy variable methods (e.g., \citealp{olley1996}; \citealp{levinsohn2003}; \citealp{ackerberg2015}; \citealp{wooldridge2009}) address this in micro-level firm datasets with intermediate materials data, macro panels spanning heterogeneous countries lack consistent materials data. 

Consequently, cross-country productivity research relies on dynamic panel GMM estimators. \citet{arellano1991} and \citet{blundell1998} developed first-differenced and system GMM estimators that purge unobserved time-invariant individual effects and exploit lagged levels and differences as internal instruments. \citet{roodman2009} outlines best practices for instrument proliferation and collapsed weighting matrices. For the secondary regression linking technology to TFP, \citet{goldsmith2020} provide the definitive identification analysis for shift-share (Bartik) instruments, formalizing the orthogonality requirements of initial shares and exogenous aggregate shifters.

\section{Theoretical Framework}
\label{sec:theory}
% =====================================================================

To provide microfoundations for our empirical design, we integrate the task-based framework of \citet{acemoglu2018} into an aggregate Solow--Jorgenson growth accounting decomposition.

\subsection{Task-Based Production and Technology}
Let aggregate output in country $c$, sector $s$, and period $t$ be produced via a continuum of tasks $i \in [N-1, N]$:
\begin{equation}
    Y_{cst} = \Omega_{cst} \left[ \int_{N-1}^{N} y_{cst}(i)^{\frac{\sigma - 1}{\sigma}} \, di \right]^{\frac{\sigma}{\sigma - 1}},
    \label{eq:task_agg}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma \in (0, \infty)$ is the elasticity of substitution between tasks and $\Omega_{cst}$ represents Hicks-neutral baseline efficiency. Each task $i$ can be produced using capital $k_{cst}(i)$ or labour $l_{cst}(i)$ according to the production technology:
\begin{equation}
    y_{cst}(i) = 
    \begin{cases} 
        \gamma_K(i) k_{cst}(i) + \gamma_L(i) l_{cst}(i) & \text{if } i \le I_{cst}, \\
        \gamma_L(i) l_{cst}(i) & \text{if } i > I_{cst},
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:task_tech}
\end{equation}
where $I_{cst} \in [N-1, N]$ denotes the threshold of automated tasks. Tasks $i \le I_{cst}$ are technologically automated and can be executed by algorithmic or physical capital, whereas tasks $i > I_{cst}$ strictly require human labour.

Adoption of artificial intelligence operates through two distinct channels:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Automation effect ($\Delta I_{cst} > 0$):} Expanding the threshold of tasks performed by capital.
    \item \textbf{Task creation and deepening ($\Delta N_{cst} > 0$, $\Delta \gamma_K > 0$):} Introducing novel complex tasks where labour has a comparative advantage, or deepening capital efficiency in already-automated tasks.
\end{enumerate}

Assuming competitive factor markets with rental rate of capital $R_{cst}$ and wage $W_{cst}$, cost minimization implies that capital is allocated to tasks where the effective cost of capital is strictly lower than labour: $R_{cst}/\gamma_K(i) < W_{cst}/\gamma_L(i)$. Under constant returns across tasks and Cobb--Douglas aggregation ($\sigma \to 1$), aggregate sectoral output simplifies to the structural function:
\begin{equation}
    Y_{cst} = A_{cst} K_{cst}^{\alpha} L_{cst}^{\beta},
    \label{eq:cd_structural}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ reflect the aggregate output elasticities of capital and labour, and $A_{cst}$ denotes true total factor productivity.

\subsection{Intangible Investment and the Productivity J-Curve}
Following \citet{brynjolfsson2021}, deploying AI requires unmeasured intangible capital accumulation, denoted $J_{cst}$. In any period $t$, a fraction of measured labour and capital is diverted away from producing final output toward building organizational and digital infrastructure:
\begin{equation}
    Y_{cst}^{\text{measured}} = Y_{cst} - \psi(I_{cst}, \Delta AI_{cst}), \quad \psi > 0, \quad \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial \Delta AI} > 0,
\end{equation}
where $\psi(\cdot)$ represents organizational installation and retraining frictions. The stock of intangible capital accumulates according to $J_{cst} = (1 - \delta_J) J_{cs,t-1} + \lambda AI_{cs,t-1}$. Over time, this unmeasured capital enhances true productivity:
\begin{equation}
    \ln A_{cst} = \ln \bar{A}_{cst} + \phi J_{cst}.
\end{equation}
Taking first differences yields the central testable prediction for measured TFP growth:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \ln A_{cst}^{\text{measured}} = \delta_0 + \underbrace{\theta_0 \Delta AI_{cst}}_{\text{Contemporaneous friction } (\theta_0 \le 0)} + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \underbrace{\theta_k AI_{cs,t-k}}_{\text{Productivity realization } (\theta_k > 0)} + \bm{\gamma}' \mathbf{X}_{cst} + \varepsilon_{cst}.
    \label{eq:jcurve_hyp}
\end{equation}

This theoretical formulation yields three core hypotheses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 1 (J-Curve Trajectory):} Contemporaneous AI adoption has an insignificant or negative association with measured TFP ($\theta_0 \le 0$), followed by positive lagged returns ($\sum_{k=1}^K \theta_k > 0$).
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 2 (Digital Complementarity):} The productivity elasticity of AI is higher in economies with mature pre-existing digital infrastructure.
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 3 (Sectoral Divergence):} Due to varying cognitive routine densities, industrial manufacturing and tech-intensive services realize productivity gains faster than primary sectors.
\end{itemize}

\section{Empirical Strategy}
\label{sec:methods}
% =====================================================================

Our empirical identification proceeds in two sequential econometric stages.

\subsection{Stage 1: Structural Production Function Estimation}
Taking natural logarithms of equation~\eqref{eq:cd_structural}:
\begin{equation}
    \ln Y_{cst} = \mu_{cs} + \tau_t + \alpha \ln K_{cst} + \beta \ln L_{cst} + \omega_{cst} + \nu_{cst},
    \label{eq:stage1_spec}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_{cs}$ represents country-sector fixed effects capturing time-invariant geographic, institutional, and structural endowments; $\tau_t$ represents year fixed effects capturing common macroeconomic and global technological shocks; $\omega_{cst}$ is transmitted productivity unobserved by the econometrician; and $\nu_{cst}$ is classical idiosyncratic measurement noise.

\paragraph{OLS Fixed Effects (Baseline):} We initially estimate equation~\eqref{eq:stage1_spec} using within-entity panel demeaning:
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{\ln Y}_{cst} = \alpha \widetilde{\ln K}_{cst} + \beta \widetilde{\ln L}_{cst} + \tilde{\tau}_t + \tilde{\nu}_{cst},
\end{equation}
where $\widetilde{X}_{cst} = X_{cst} - \bar{X}_{cs}$. While OLS-FE absorbs $\mu_{cs}$, it yields upward-biased elasticities if inputs respond to time-varying productivity shocks $\Delta \omega_{cst}$ (transmission bias). We also estimate the constant returns to scale (CRS, $\alpha + \beta = 1$) restricted specification in per-worker form:
\begin{equation}
    \ln \left( \frac{Y_{cst}}{L_{cst}} \right) = \mu_{cs} + \tau_t + \alpha \ln \left( \frac{K_{cst}}{L_{cst}} \right) + u_{cst},
\end{equation}
and formally test the restriction via a Wald test on the unrestricted parameters.

\paragraph{First-Differenced IV (Primary Estimator):} To eliminate $\mu_{cs}$ and resolve simultaneity, we apply the first-difference operator $\Delta X_{cst} = X_{cst} - X_{cs,t-1}$:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \ln Y_{cst} = \Delta \tau_t + \alpha \Delta \ln K_{cst} + \beta \Delta \ln L_{cst} + \Delta \omega_{cst} + \Delta \nu_{cst}.
    \label{eq:iv_fd}
\end{equation}
Because input growth rates $\Delta \ln K_{cst}$ and $\Delta \ln L_{cst}$ remain correlated with the differenced error term $(\nu_{cst} - \nu_{cs,t-1})$, we instrument $(\Delta \ln K_{cst}, \Delta \ln L_{cst})$ using predetermined second-lagged levels $(\ln K_{cs,t-2}, \ln L_{cs,t-2})$ following \citet{anderson1982} and \citet{arellano1991}. Under the standard assumption that idiosyncratic shocks are serially uncorrelated ($E[\nu_{cst} \nu_{cs,t-s}] = 0$ for $s \ge 1$), lagged levels at $t-2$ are orthogonal to the differenced error:
\begin{equation}
    E[\ln K_{cs,t-2} \cdot (\Delta \omega_{cst} + \Delta \nu_{cst})] = 0, \quad E[\ln L_{cs,t-2} \cdot (\Delta \omega_{cst} + \Delta \nu_{cst})] = 0.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{System GMM (Robustness):} As shown by \citet{blundell1998}, when capital and labour series exhibit high persistence near unit roots, lagged levels become weak instruments for first differences. We therefore estimate a dynamic System GMM model that stacks the first-differenced equations (instrumented by lagged levels) with the levels equations (instrumented by lagged differences):
\begin{equation}
    \ln Y_{cst} = \rho \ln Y_{cs,t-1} + \alpha \ln K_{cst} + \beta \ln L_{cst} + \mu_{cs} + \tau_t + \varepsilon_{cst}.
\end{equation}
To prevent instrument proliferation, we collapse the instrument matrix following \citet{roodman2009} and restrict maximum lag depth to 4 periods ($GMM\_MAX\_LAGS = 4$). We report the \citet{hansen1982} test of overidentifying restrictions and the Arellano--Bond AR(1) and AR(2) autocorrelation tests in first differences.

\paragraph{Growth Accounting Decomposition:} Using the preferred consistent elasticities $(\hat{\alpha}, \hat{\beta})$, annual output growth is decomposed into capital contribution $C_K$, labour contribution $C_L$, and the Solow residual $\Delta \ln A_{cst}$:
\begin{align}
    C_{K,cst} &= \hat{\alpha} \cdot \Delta \ln K_{cst}, \label{eq:ck} \\
    C_{L,cst} &= \hat{\beta} \cdot \Delta \ln L_{cst}, \label{eq:cl} \\
    \Delta \ln A_{cst} &= \Delta \ln Y_{cst} - C_{K,cst} - C_{L,cst}. \label{eq:tfp_res}
\end{align}

\subsection{Stage 2: Causal AI $\to$ TFP Model and Bartik Identification}
The second stage investigates whether enterprise AI adoption drives variation in residual TFP growth $\Delta \ln A_{cst}$:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \ln A_{cst} = \delta + \theta AI_{ct} + \bm{\gamma}' \mathbf{X}_{ct} + \mu_{cs} + \tau_t + \varepsilon_{cst},
    \label{eq:stage2_ols}
\end{equation}
where $AI_{ct}$ denotes the percentage of enterprises adopting AI in country $c$ and year $t$, and $\mathbf{X}_{ct}$ is a vector of time-varying macroeconomic controls: log real GDP per capita, gross tertiary education enrolment ratio, research and development (R\&D) expenditure as a percentage of GDP, trade openness ($(\text{Exports} + \text{Imports})/\text{GDP}$), and general internet user penetration. To address potential generated-regressor variance in the dependent variable, standard errors are clustered at the country level \citep{moulton1990}.

\paragraph{The Bartik-Style Instrumental Variable:} Even after conditioning on high-dimensional fixed effects and control covariates, OLS estimates of $\theta$ in equation~\eqref{eq:stage2_ols} may suffer from omitted variable bias and reverse causality. We construct a Bartik-style shift-share instrument $Z_{ct}$ defined as:
\begin{equation}
    Z_{ct} = \text{Broadband}_{c, 2010} \times \Delta \overline{AI}_{-c, t},
    \label{eq:bartik_def}
\end{equation}
where $\text{Broadband}_{c, 2010}$ is fixed broadband subscriptions per 100 people in country $c$ in the pre-sample base year 2010 (predating modern commercial deep learning and transformer architectures), and $\Delta \overline{AI}_{-c, t}$ is the global leave-one-out average AI adoption rate across all sample countries excluding country $c$:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \overline{AI}_{-c, t} = \frac{1}{M_t - 1} \sum_{j \ne c} AI_{jt}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Identifying Assumptions:} Causal identification through $Z_{ct}$ rests on two core econometric assumptions formalized by \citet{goldsmith2020}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Relevance Condition ($Cov(Z_{ct}, AI_{ct} \mid \mathbf{X}_{ct}, \mu_{cs}, \tau_t) \ne 0$):} Economies with superior high-speed broadband infrastructure prior to the modern AI revolution were uniquely positioned to scale cloud-based neural networks and machine learning APIs rapidly when global models emerged, guaranteeing a strong first stage ($F > 10$).
    \item \textbf{Exclusion Restriction ($Cov(Z_{ct}, \varepsilon_{cst} \mid \mathbf{X}_{ct}, \mu_{cs}, \tau_t) = 0$):} Historical fixed broadband penetration in 2010 affects annual sectoral TFP growth in the 2020s \emph{only} through its facilitating impact on modern AI adoption, once contemporaneous internet penetration, R\&D spending, tertiary education, and country-sector fixed effects are directly controlled for. The leave-one-out construction ensures that local idiosyncratic productivity shocks cannot reflect into the aggregate global trend.
\end{enumerate}

We estimate the second-stage IV equation via Two-Stage Least Squares (IV-2SLS) and Limited Information Maximum Likelihood (LIML), which is robust to weak instrument bias in finite samples.

\paragraph{Distributed-Lag Model:} To test Hypothesis~1 (the J-curve), we estimate distributed-lag horizons spanning 0 to 3 years:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \ln A_{cst} = \delta + \sum_{k=0}^{K} \theta_k AI_{c, t-k} + \bm{\gamma}' \mathbf{X}_{ct} + \mu_{cs} + \tau_t + \varepsilon_{cst}, \quad K \in \{1, 2, 3\}.
    \label{eq:dist_lag}
\end{equation}
The cumulative medium-run productivity return across horizon $K$ is given by $\Theta_K = \sum_{k=0}^K \hat{\theta}_k$.

\subsection{Resolving the Capital Allocation Flaw}
A primary methodological flaw in prior cross-country sectoral productivity research has been the practice of allocating aggregate national capital stocks to sectors in proportion to sectoral employment shares: $K_{cst} = K_{ct} \times (L_{cst} / L_{ct})$. This naive assumption mathematically forces the capital-to-labour ratio to be identical across all sectors within a country-year:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{K_{cst}}{L_{cst}} = \frac{K_{ct} \cdot (L_{cst}/L_{ct})}{L_{cst}} = \frac{K_{ct}}{L_{ct}}, \quad \forall s \in \{\text{Agri, Ind, Svc}\}.
\end{equation}
Under this constraint, capital intensity variation between highly capital-intensive industries (e.g., automated manufacturing, chemical refining) and labour-intensive services or subsistence agriculture is completely eliminated, distorting production elasticity estimates.

To resolve this, we implement a two-step structural allocation procedure:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{National Capital Benchmarking:} We extract national capital stocks $K_c^{\text{PWT}}$ in millions of constant 2017 national prices from the Penn World Table (PWT 10.01, \texttt{rnna}), which applies rigorous asset-specific perpetual inventory models across ten asset categories \citep{feenstra2015}. Because PWT 10.01 coverage concludes in 2019, we extend national stocks through 2024 using World Bank constant 2015 GFCF ($I_{ct}^{\text{WB}}$) via perpetual inventory with depreciation $\delta = 0.05$:
    \begin{equation}
        K_{c, t+1} = (1 - \delta) K_{ct} + \kappa_c \cdot I_{c, t+1}^{\text{WB}}, \quad \forall t \ge 2019,
    \end{equation}
    where $\kappa_c = K_{c, 2019}^{\text{PWT}} / I_{c, 2019}^{\text{WB}}$ aligns national currency units and price bases.
    \item \textbf{Value-Added Composition Allocation:} National capital is allocated to sectors according to sectoral gross value-added shares:
    \begin{equation}
        K_{cst} = K_{ct} \times \left( \frac{Y_{cst}}{\sum_{j} Y_{cjt}} \right),
    \end{equation}
    permitting sectoral capital-labour ratios $K_{cst}/L_{cst}$ to vary endogenously with sectoral productivity, output composition, and capital intensity.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{1. Translog Production Function:} To test whether our Stage 1 results are artifacts of Cobb--Douglas unitary elasticity of substitution ($\sigma = 1$), we estimate a generalized transcendental logarithmic (translog) specification:
\begin{equation}
    \ln Y_{cst} = \mu_{cs} + \tau_t + \alpha_1 \ln K + \alpha_2 \ln L + \alpha_3 (\ln K)^2 + \alpha_4 (\ln L)^2 + \alpha_5 (\ln K \times \ln L) + u_{cst}.
\end{equation}
Evaluating point elasticities at sample means yields $\alpha_K = 0.5902$ and $\beta_L = 0.1926$ (sum $= 0.7827$), nearly identical to our baseline OLS-FE estimates ($0.5989$ and $0.1807$). The interaction coefficient $\alpha_5$ is small ($0.0093$), demonstrating that Cobb--Douglas constitutes an empirically valid approximation of the underlying sectoral technology.

\paragraph{2. OECD Subsample Stability:} Restricting the estimation sample strictly to OECD member countries ($N = 324$) yields a positive coefficient of $\hat{\theta} = +0.1208$ ($SE = 0.1244$). While statistically insignificant at conventional levels, the point estimate is eight times larger than the full global sample, reinforcing the evidence that productivity returns are concentrated in technologically mature economies.

\paragraph{3. Falsification and Placebo Evaluation:} We perform a placebo test by assigning artificial randomized AI adoption rates during the historical pre-adoption period (prior to 2014) and re-estimating the Stage 2 model. The placebo coefficient is $\hat{\theta}_{\text{fake}} = 0.1248$ with a $p$-value of $0.4460$. The failure of artificial adoption to predict historical TFP growth confirms that our second-stage identification does not capture pre-existing secular trends.

\paragraph{4. Leave-One-Out Country Jackknife:} To ensure that results are not driven by outlier nations (e.g., the United States or large tech hubs), we execute a complete leave-one-out jackknife procedure, dropping each country iteratively ($n = 37$). The jackknife mean coefficient is $\hat{\theta}_{\text{JK}} = 0.0650$ with empirical standard error $0.1320$, spanning a narrow range from $-0.0112$ to $0.1156$. No single nation exercises disproportionate leverage over the estimated elasticity.

\section{Discussion and Policy Implications}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Reconciling Macro Insignificance with Micro Optimism}
Our empirical finding of a statistically negligible aggregate TFP impact ($\hat{\theta}_{\text{IV}} = -0.034$) stands in apparent contrast with firm-level studies documenting double-digit productivity advantages for early AI adopters \citep{brynjolfsson2023, babina2024}. This divergence can be reconciled through three economic mechanisms:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Business Stealing vs. Frontier Expansion:} In competitive markets, early adopting firms capture market share from non-adopting rivals without expanding the aggregate production frontier. As \citet{acemoglu2024} demonstrates, when micro-level firm gains represent redistribution of rents and revenues within an industry, the macroeconomic TFP dividend is a fraction of micro-level returns.
    \item \textbf{Limited Aggregate Task Exposure:} Even with high enterprise adoption percentages, AI systems currently automate only a narrow subset of routine cognitive tasks. In our OECD panel, enterprise adoption averages between 8 and 30 percent across member states, but within adopting firms, AI tools touch only a small fraction of total worker-hours.
    \item \textbf{Unmeasured Intangible Capital Divergence:} In accordance with \citet{corrado2021} and \citet{brynjolfsson2021}, substantial productive resources are currently absorbed by organizational restructuring, data cleaning, and workflow design. Because these activities represent intermediate costs under standard accounting rules rather than final output, measured productivity is temporarily depressed.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Policy Implications}
These findings offer several key implications for economic policymakers and national statistical agencies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Infrastructure Before Adoption:} The sharp contrast between high-income economies ($\hat{\theta} = +0.0155$) and emerging economies ($\hat{\theta} = -14.105$) demonstrates that subsidizing enterprise software adoption in the absence of complementary digital infrastructure (broadband, cloud computing, cybersecurity) and technical literacy generates substantial economic waste.
    \item \textbf{Modernizing National Accounts for Intangibles:} Statistical agencies should accelerate efforts to capitalize intangible data assets and organizational investments. Without accurate measurement of intangible capital accumulation, productivity statistics will continue to misdiagnose the early stages of technological revolutions as economic stagnation.
    \item \textbf{Targeting Task Creation Over Displacement:} Because industrial automation yields positive gains ($\hat{\theta} = +0.033$) while routine service automation yields negative returns ($\hat{\theta} = -0.027$), public innovation policies should incentivize AI applications that augment human decision-making and create new productive tasks rather than merely displacing clerical and customer service workers.
\end{itemize}

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =====================================================================

This study provides a comprehensive, multi-country empirical evaluation of the macroeconomic productivity contribution of artificial intelligence, formally separating AI adoption from capital and labour deepening. Combining Penn World Table 10.01 capital stocks, OECD enterprise AI metrics, and World Bank indicators across 166 countries and three productive sectors from 2000 to 2024, we employ a two-stage structural design featuring first-differenced instrumental variables, System GMM, and a Bartik shift-share instrument based on historical broadband penetration.

Our central empirical conclusion is that enterprise AI adoption has not yet generated a broad-based, statistically significant acceleration in aggregate total factor productivity ($\hat{\theta} = +0.015$, IV $\hat{\theta} = -0.034$). Instead, the contemporary economy exhibits the classic hallmarks of a General Purpose Technology in its early adoption phase: initial transitional frictions consistent with the Productivity J-Curve, positive returns concentrated narrowly in technologically advanced OECD nations and industrial manufacturing, and transitional costs in developing economies. As frontier models mature and complementary organizational capital accumulates, future research should track whether this initial gestation phase gives way to the anticipated aggregate productivity dividend.

\subsection*{A.1 Data Cleaning, Winsorization, and Sample Balance}
All monetary variables drawn from the World Bank (value added, gross fixed capital formation, GDP per capita) are denominated in constant 2015 US dollars, while Penn World Table capital stocks are in constant 2017 national currency prices aligned through 2019 baseline overlap scaling factors $\kappa_c = K_{c, 2019}^{\text{PWT}} / I_{c, 2019}^{\text{WB}}$. To eliminate distortion from extreme measurement noise and macroeconomic crises in developing nations, estimated Solow TFP growth residuals $\Delta \ln A_{cst}$ are winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles ($WINSOR\_PCTILES = (0.01, 0.99)$). Entities with non-positive values for real output, capital, or labour are removed prior to logarithmic transformation.

\subsection*{A.2 Computational Pipeline and Open-Source Replication Package}
The entire empirical pipeline is executed in a self-contained, modular Python architecture structured across 11 modules:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \texttt{config.py}: Global parameter definitions, API endpoints, time windows, and winsorization thresholds.
    \item \texttt{utils.py}: Cached REST ingestion utilities, console-to-disk logging tee, and panel demeaning functions.
    \item \texttt{api\_worldbank.py}: Automated batch extraction of WDI sectoral value added, employment, and macroeconomic controls.
    \item \texttt{api\_oecd.py}: OECD SDMX REST parser extracting enterprise AI adoption rates (\texttt{DSD\_ICT\_B}).
    \item \texttt{api\_pwt.py}: Automated download and parsing of Penn World Table 10.01 capital stocks (\texttt{rnna}) and human capital.
    \item \texttt{panel.py}: Harmonization of international series into balanced panel with value-added capital allocation and Bartik instrument construction.
    \item \texttt{stage1.py}: Structural production function estimation (OLS-FE, IV-FD, and System GMM) and Solow residual decomposition.
    \item \texttt{stage2.py}: Causal regression of TFP growth on enterprise AI adoption (OLS-FE, Bartik IV-2SLS, LIML, and distributed lags).
    \item \texttt{robustness.py}: Comprehensive battery (translog production function, OECD subsample, placebo pre-period test, and leave-one-out jackknife).
    \item \texttt{reporting.py}: Automated generation of publication-quality booktabs LaTeX tables, CSV files, and vector PDF figures.
    \item \texttt{run_analysis.py}: Master pipeline orchestrator supporting live API updates and offline validation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{A.3 Software Dependencies and Replication Protocol}
The econometric analysis is implemented in Python~3.12 utilizing \texttt{pandas} (2.2.3), \texttt{numpy} (2.1.3), \texttt{linearmodels} (7.0), \texttt{statsmodels} (0.14.4), \texttt{scipy} (1.14.1), \texttt{pydynpd} (0.4.0), \texttt{requests} (2.32.3), and \texttt{openpyxl} (3.1.5). The complete replication package, including cached data and raw logs, is provided in the accompanying repository. Running \texttt{python run\_analysis.py} from the \texttt{code/} directory reproduces all empirical tables and figures directly.
