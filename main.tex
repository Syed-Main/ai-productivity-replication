\begin{abstract}
\noindent A central question in modern macroeconomics is whether enterprise adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) generates genuine total factor productivity (TFP) gains or merely reflects capital deepening and workforce reallocation. This study develops a two-stage empirical identification framework that formally disentangles AI-induced efficiency improvements from conventional capital and labour contributions across 166 countries between 2000 and 2024. In the first stage, we resolve Marschak--Andrews transmission bias and returns-to-scale distortions by implementing standard Solow--Jorgenson Törnqvist growth accounting anchored in empirical time-varying labor shares ($labsh_{ct}$) from Penn World Table 10.01, alongside a one-step joint production function specification where AI enters directly alongside capital and labor. National capital stocks are directly benchmarked to PWT 10.01 constant-price series (\texttt{rnna}) and extended beyond 2019 by applying the annual real growth rate of World Bank constant-price gross fixed capital formation to the 2019 PWT real investment level via perpetual inventory. In the second stage, we evaluate aggregate country-year TFP growth alongside a Rajan--Zingales sectoral task-exposure mechanism model with country-by-year and sector-by-year fixed effects. To resolve endogenous technology adoption and reverse causality, we construct an overidentified shift-share instrumental variable system interacting pre-existing 2010 fixed broadband ($Z_1$) and internet penetration ($Z_2$) with global leave-one-out AI diffusion trends, evaluated via 2SLS, Limited Information Maximum Likelihood (LIML), Hansen $J$ overidentification tests, and Anderson--Rubin weak-instrument-robust confidence tests. Our empirical findings indicate that contemporaneous enterprise AI adoption yields an economically modest and statistically insignificant direct impact on aggregate TFP ($\hat{\theta} = +0.005$, $p = 0.926$; IV-2SLS $\hat{\theta} = +0.153$, $p = 0.415$; IV-LIML $\hat{\theta} = +0.299$, $p = 0.483$), while distributed lags reflect initial adoption frictions followed by positive gestation returns ($\hat{\theta}_1 = +0.054, \hat{\theta}_2 = +0.096$). Subsample and heterogeneity analyses confirm that productivity returns are concentrated in digitally mature economies and industrial sectors. A comprehensive battery of robustness tests---including translog production functions, placebo historical falsification tests, and leave-one-out jackknife estimations---verifies the stability of our findings.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =====================================================================

The rapid diffusion of artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning, and algorithmic automation across enterprise workflows has re-ignited intense debate regarding the macroeconomic trajectory of modern productivity. Proponents argue that generative and analytical AI represents the quintessential general purpose technology (GPT) of the twenty-first century, poised to break decades of secular stagnation by automating cognitive routines, accelerating scientific discovery, and shifting the aggregate production frontier outward \citep{brynjolfsson2017, ager2023, babina2024}. Conversely, sceptics emphasize that despite pervasive adoption headlines, aggregate total factor productivity (TFP) growth across advanced and emerging economies remains historically subdued, echoing Robert Solow's famous 1987 aphorism that computers could be seen everywhere except in the productivity statistics \citep{gordon2016, acemoglu2024}.

Resolving this dispute empirically at the macroeconomic level presents severe econometric and conceptual difficulties. An observed expansion in gross output coinciding with the installation of enterprise AI systems cannot legitimately be attributed to an AI-driven productivity shock. Output growth may mechanically stem from rapid capital deepening (expenditures on specialized graphics processing units, server racks, cloud computing architecture, and data storage), parallel expansions in skilled labour inputs, or unobserved demand and macroeconomic cycles. Furthermore, national income and product accounts (NIPA) record hardware and software investments as tangible and intangible gross fixed capital formation, while organizational redesign, bespoke algorithmic fine-tuning, workflow adjustments, and internal data curation are rarely capitalized, obscuring the boundary between input accumulation and technological efficiency \citep{corrado2021}. 

This measurement challenge is compounded by two fundamental endogeneity problems. First, in estimating the underlying production elasticities of capital and labour, firms adjust variable inputs in response to productivity shocks observed by managers but unobserved by econometricians---the classical transmission bias identified by \citet{marschak1944}. Second, at the secondary stage of estimating the returns to technological adoption, AI adoption itself is highly endogenous: high-performing sectors and frontier economies with superior institutional capacity, higher human capital, and stronger initial digital infrastructure adopt AI faster, generating severe confounding and reverse causality. Naive Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regressions of productivity on AI metrics inevitably yield biased estimates.

This paper develops a rigorous econometric framework designed to identify the causal productivity contribution of artificial intelligence while cleanly separating it from capital and labour deepening. Our methodology departs from existing cross-country productivity studies in five critical dimensions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Aggregate Country-Year Panel Architecture with True Capital Stocks:} We estimate primary macroeconomic production and productivity equations at the aggregate country-year level where Penn World Table (PWT 10.01) capital stocks (\texttt{rnna}) are directly measured, avoiding artificial sectoral value-added share allocation distortions. To extend PWT \texttt{rnna} past 2019, we apply the annual real growth rate of World Bank constant-price gross fixed capital formation (GFCF) to the 2019 PWT real investment series ($I_{2019}^{\text{PWT}} = csh\_i_{2019} \times Y_{2019}^{\text{PWT}}$) and accumulate capital via perpetual inventory ($K_t = (1-\delta)K_{t-1} + I_t$).
    \item \textbf{Cost-Share Törnqvist Residuals and One-Step Joint Estimation:} Rather than computing TFP residuals from imprecise econometric regressions (e.g., Anderson--Hsiao models yielding explosive elasticities), we compute standard Solow--Jorgenson Törnqvist residuals using time-varying labor shares ($1 - \alpha_{ct} = labsh_{ct}$) from PWT 10.01. We complement this two-step residual approach with a one-step joint structural production function where $AI_{ct}$ enters directly alongside $\ln K$ and $\ln L$.
    \item \textbf{Aligned Stage 2 Aggregation and Sectoral Exposure Mechanisms:} We execute Stage 2 regressions at the aggregate country-year level ($N = 102$ across 36 countries) and test micro-mechanisms via a Rajan--Zingales interaction model that interacts national AI adoption with baseline sectoral AI task exposure ($AI_{cst} = AI_{ct} \times \text{Exposure}_s$) while saturating the specification with country-by-year and sector-by-year fixed effects.
    \item \textbf{Overidentified Instrumental Variables and Weak-Instrument Robustness:} We construct an overidentified system of two predetermined shift-share instruments ($Z_1$ from 2010 fixed broadband penetration and $Z_2$ from 2010 internet penetration, each interacted with leave-one-out global AI diffusion). This allows us to estimate Limited Information Maximum Likelihood (LIML) alongside 2SLS, report Hansen $J$ overidentification tests ($df=1$), and compute Anderson--Rubin weak-instrument-robust confidence tests.
    \item \textbf{Sample Count Reconciliation and Diagnostic Discipline:} We audit and harmonize control selection across all modules, ensuring the OECD subsample ($N = 92$) is a strict, logically consistent subset of the full estimation sample ($N = 102$). We suppress uninformative negative $R^2$ statistics in IV tables and reconcile empirical findings with the distributed-lag J-curve trajectory.
\end{enumerate}

Previewing our primary empirical results, the empirical Solow--Jorgenson factor shares average $\bar{\alpha} = 0.489$ for capital and $\bar{\beta} = 0.511$ for labor. When Törnqvist TFP residuals are regressed on enterprise AI adoption, contemporaneous AI exerts an economically negligible direct effect ($\hat{\theta}_{\text{OLS}} = +0.005$, $p = 0.926$; overidentified IV-2SLS $\hat{\theta}_{\text{IV}} = +0.153$, $p = 0.415$; LIML $\hat{\theta}_{\text{LIML}} = +0.299$, $p = 0.483$), with Hansen $J$ test diagnostics failing to reject instrument validity ($p = 0.071$). Over multi-year horizons, distributed-lag models indicate that initial adoption frictions give way to positive gestation returns ($\hat{\theta}_1 = +0.054, \hat{\theta}_2 = +0.096$). Placebo falsification tests ($p = 0.833$), translog production estimations, and leave-one-out jackknife evaluations verify the robustness of these conclusions.

\section{Related Literature}
\label{sec:lit}
% =====================================================================

Our research intersects four active strands of the economic literature: growth accounting and general purpose technologies, microeconomic evidence on artificial intelligence, macroeconomic growth projections, and the econometrics of panel production functions.

\subsection{General Purpose Technologies and Growth Accounting}
The foundational architecture for measuring productivity originates from \citet{solow1957} and was operationalized into comprehensive multi-factor accounting by \citet{jorgenson1967}. In this neoclassical growth accounting paradigm, output growth is decomposed into input growth weighted by marginal factor shares, with the unexplained residual interpreted as costless shifts in the aggregate production frontier (total factor productivity). 

When applied to major technological transitions, however, standard growth accounting often encounters the paradox identified by \citet{bresnahan1995}: general purpose technologies (such as the steam engine, electrification, and digital computing) generate profound economic transformations that fail to appear in concurrent productivity statistics. As \citet{stiroh2002} and \citet{jorgenson2008} demonstrated in the context of the 1990s Information and Communication Technology (ICT) revolution, measured productivity accelerations materialized only after sustained capital deepening in computing hardware was paired with sweeping organizational and complementary structural adjustments. \citet{gordon2016} cautions, however, that digital innovations may exhibit diminishing marginal economic returns compared to the transformative Great Inventions of the second industrial revolution.

\subsection{Microeconomic Evidence on Artificial Intelligence}
In contrast to macroeconomic aggregates, recent microeconomic and firm-level research documents tangible benefits from AI adoption. Using administrative and job-posting data, \citet{babina2024} show that AI-investing firms experience faster growth in sales, employment, and market valuation, driven primarily by product innovation rather than cost displacement. Similarly, \citet{brynjolfsson2023} conduct a randomized control study of generative AI customer support assistants, finding a 14 percent average boost in issue resolution per hour, with the largest gains accruing to novice and lower-skilled workers. \citet{alderucci2020} document that patenting in AI technologies is associated with substantial firm-level revenue and employment premiums. 

Nevertheless, microeconomic productivity premiums do not automatically aggregate into macroeconomic expansions. As \citet{acemoglu2018, acemoglu2020} emphasize in their task-based framework, if technological adoption is characterized by ``so-so automation''---substituting capital for labour in routine tasks with only marginal cost reductions without creating new high-productivity tasks---aggregate economic growth remains tepid while labour share declines.

\subsection{Macroeconomic Assessments and the Productivity J-Curve}
At the aggregate level, \citet{acemoglu2024} develops a formal macroeconomic evaluation of AI exposure, calculating that fewer than 5 percent of economic tasks can be cost-effectively automated by computer vision and large language models over a ten-year horizon, translating into a modest cumulative TFP increase of no more than 0.55 percent (or less than 0.06 percent annually). In complementary work, \citet{filippucci2024} evaluate OECD cross-country exposure and find substantial heterogeneity based on industrial structure and technological readiness.

The primary explanation for delayed macroeconomic impacts is the \emph{Productivity J-Curve} formalized by \citet{brynjolfsson2021}. They demonstrate that when a new GPT emerges, firms must redirect substantial capital and labour toward creating intangible assets---reorganizing management structures, retraining personnel, accumulating proprietary datasets, and rewiring business workflows. Because national accounts count this diverted effort as input expenditures while omitting the resulting unmeasured intangible capital from output, measured productivity growth initially falls or stagnates. Only in subsequent periods, when intangible capital begins delivering productive services without further adjustment costs, does measured TFP accelerate.

\subsection{Identification in Panel Production Functions}
Estimating production functions is plagued by endogeneity. Since \citet{marschak1944}, econometricians have recognized that profit-maximizing firms adjust input demands in response to anticipated productivity shocks, creating positive correlation between regressors and the error term. While structural proxy variable methods (e.g., \citealp{olley1996}; \citealp{levinsohn2003}; \citealp{ackerberg2015}; \citealp{wooldridge2009}) address this in micro-level firm datasets with intermediate materials data, macro panels spanning heterogeneous countries lack consistent materials data. 

Consequently, cross-country productivity research relies on dynamic panel GMM estimators. \citet{arellano1991} and \citet{blundell1998} developed first-differenced and system GMM estimators that purge unobserved time-invariant individual effects and exploit lagged levels and differences as internal instruments. \citet{roodman2009} outlines best practices for instrument proliferation and collapsed weighting matrices. For the secondary regression linking technology to TFP, \citet{goldsmith2020} provide the definitive identification analysis for shift-share (Bartik) instruments, formalizing the orthogonality requirements of initial shares and exogenous aggregate shifters.

\section{Theoretical Framework}
\label{sec:theory}
% =====================================================================

To provide microfoundations for our empirical design, we integrate the task-based framework of \citet{acemoglu2018} into an aggregate Solow--Jorgenson growth accounting decomposition.

\subsection{Task-Based Production and Technology}
Let aggregate output in country $c$, sector $s$, and period $t$ be produced via a continuum of tasks $i \in [N-1, N]$:
\begin{equation}
    Y_{cst} = \Omega_{cst} \left[ \int_{N-1}^{N} y_{cst}(i)^{\frac{\sigma - 1}{\sigma}} \, di \right]^{\frac{\sigma}{\sigma - 1}},
    \label{eq:task_agg}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma \in (0, \infty)$ is the elasticity of substitution between tasks and $\Omega_{cst}$ represents Hicks-neutral baseline efficiency. Each task $i$ can be produced using capital $k_{cst}(i)$ or labour $l_{cst}(i)$ according to the production technology:
\begin{equation}
    y_{cst}(i) = 
    \begin{cases} 
        \gamma_K(i) k_{cst}(i) + \gamma_L(i) l_{cst}(i) & \text{if } i \le I_{cst}, \\
        \gamma_L(i) l_{cst}(i) & \text{if } i > I_{cst},
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:task_tech}
\end{equation}
where $I_{cst} \in [N-1, N]$ denotes the threshold of automated tasks. Tasks $i \le I_{cst}$ are technologically automated and can be executed by algorithmic or physical capital, whereas tasks $i > I_{cst}$ strictly require human labour.

Adoption of artificial intelligence operates through two distinct channels:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Automation effect ($\Delta I_{cst} > 0$):} Expanding the threshold of tasks performed by capital.
    \item \textbf{Task creation and deepening ($\Delta N_{cst} > 0$, $\Delta \gamma_K > 0$):} Introducing novel complex tasks where labour has a comparative advantage, or deepening capital efficiency in already-automated tasks.
\end{enumerate}

Assuming competitive factor markets with rental rate of capital $R_{cst}$ and wage $W_{cst}$, cost minimization implies that capital is allocated to tasks where the effective cost of capital is strictly lower than labour: $R_{cst}/\gamma_K(i) < W_{cst}/\gamma_L(i)$. Under constant returns across tasks and Cobb--Douglas aggregation ($\sigma \to 1$), aggregate sectoral output simplifies to the structural function:
\begin{equation}
    Y_{cst} = A_{cst} K_{cst}^{\alpha} L_{cst}^{\beta},
    \label{eq:cd_structural}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ reflect the aggregate output elasticities of capital and labour, and $A_{cst}$ denotes true total factor productivity.

\subsection{Intangible Investment and the Productivity J-Curve}
Following \citet{brynjolfsson2021}, deploying AI requires unmeasured intangible capital accumulation, denoted $J_{cst}$. In any period $t$, a fraction of measured labour and capital is diverted away from producing final output toward building organizational and digital infrastructure:
\begin{equation}
    Y_{cst}^{\text{measured}} = Y_{cst} - \psi(I_{cst}, \Delta AI_{cst}), \quad \psi > 0, \quad \frac{\partial \psi}{\partial \Delta AI} > 0,
\end{equation}
where $\psi(\cdot)$ represents organizational installation and retraining frictions. The stock of intangible capital accumulates according to $J_{cst} = (1 - \delta_J) J_{cs,t-1} + \lambda AI_{cs,t-1}$. Over time, this unmeasured capital enhances true productivity:
\begin{equation}
    \ln A_{cst} = \ln \bar{A}_{cst} + \phi J_{cst}.
\end{equation}
Taking first differences yields the central testable prediction for measured TFP growth:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \ln A_{cst}^{\text{measured}} = \delta_0 + \underbrace{\theta_0 \Delta AI_{cst}}_{\text{Contemporaneous friction } (\theta_0 \le 0)} + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \underbrace{\theta_k AI_{cs,t-k}}_{\text{Productivity realization } (\theta_k > 0)} + \bm{\gamma}' \mathbf{X}_{cst} + \varepsilon_{cst}.
    \label{eq:jcurve_hyp}
\end{equation}

This theoretical formulation yields three core hypotheses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 1 (J-Curve Trajectory):} Contemporaneous AI adoption has an insignificant or negative association with measured TFP ($\theta_0 \le 0$), followed by positive lagged returns ($\sum_{k=1}^K \theta_k > 0$).
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 2 (Digital Complementarity):} The productivity elasticity of AI is higher in economies with mature pre-existing digital infrastructure.
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 3 (Sectoral Divergence):} Due to varying cognitive routine densities, industrial manufacturing and tech-intensive services realize productivity gains faster than primary sectors.
\end{itemize}

\section{Empirical Strategy}
\label{sec:methods}
% =====================================================================

Our empirical identification proceeds in two sequential econometric stages, accompanied by a direct one-step joint production function benchmark.

\subsection{Stage 1: Production Functions, Factor Shares, and Growth Accounting}

\paragraph{Solow--Jorgenson Törnqvist Index (Primary Benchmark):}
Rather than deriving residual productivity from econometric production function regressions that are vulnerable to transmission bias and imprecise elasticity estimates, our primary growth accounting benchmark implements the nonparametric Solow--Jorgenson Törnqvist index \citep{jorgenson1967}. Under competitive factor markets and cost-minimizing behavior, the output elasticity of labor equals the cost share of labor, and constant returns to scale holds by construction ($\alpha_{ct} + \beta_{ct} = 1$). We extract time-varying national labor shares $labsh_{ct}$ directly from the Penn World Table (PWT 10.01), defining:
\begin{equation}
    \beta_{ct} = labsh_{ct}, \quad \alpha_{ct} = 1 - labsh_{ct}.
\end{equation}
The two-period average factor shares are defined as $\bar{\alpha}_{ct} = \frac{1}{2}(\alpha_{ct} + \alpha_{c,t-1})$ and $\bar{\beta}_{ct} = 1 - \bar{\alpha}_{ct}$. Annual output growth is decomposed into the contributions of capital accumulation ($C_{K,ct}$), labor growth ($C_{L,ct}$), and residual TFP growth ($\Delta \ln A_{ct}$):
\begin{align}
    C_{K,ct} &= \bar{\alpha}_{ct} \cdot \Delta \ln K_{ct}, \label{eq:ck_torn} \\
    C_{L,ct} &= \bar{\beta}_{ct} \cdot \Delta \ln L_{ct}, \label{eq:cl_torn} \\
    \Delta \ln A_{ct} &= \Delta \ln Y_{ct} - C_{K,ct} - C_{L,ct}. \label{eq:tfp_torn}
\end{align}
This formulation grounds the productivity residual directly in economic theory and eliminates the returns-to-scale distortions that plague unrestricted regression residuals.

\paragraph{One-Step Joint Production Function:}
As a direct structural alternative that avoids two-step generated-regressor issues entirely, we estimate a unified one-step production function where enterprise AI adoption enters the output equation directly alongside capital and labor:
\begin{equation}
    \ln Y_{ct} = \mu_c + \tau_t + \alpha \ln K_{ct} + \beta \ln L_{ct} + \theta AI_{ct} + \bm{\gamma}' \mathbf{X}_{ct} + \varepsilon_{ct},
    \label{eq:onestep}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_c$ is country fixed effects, $\tau_t$ is year fixed effects, and $\theta$ captures the direct marginal percentage output gain from a one-percentage-point increase in enterprise AI adoption, conditional on capital and labor inputs.

\paragraph{Econometric Benchmarks (OLS-FE, IV-FD, System GMM):}
For comparative validation against the growth accounting literature, we also estimate standard Cobb--Douglas specifications:
\begin{equation}
    \ln Y_{cst} = \mu_{cs} + \tau_t + \alpha \ln K_{cst} + \beta \ln L_{cst} + \omega_{cst} + \nu_{cst}.
    \label{eq:cd_bench}
\end{equation}
We estimate equation~\eqref{eq:cd_bench} via:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{OLS Fixed Effects:} Within-entity demeaning in unrestricted and per-worker CRS-restricted form ($\ln(Y/L) = \mu_{cs} + \tau_t + \alpha \ln(K/L) + u$).
    \item \textbf{First-Differenced IV (Anderson--Hsiao):} Differencing to remove $\mu_{cs}$ and instrumenting $(\Delta \ln K, \Delta \ln L)$ with second-lagged levels $(\ln K_{t-2}, \ln L_{t-2})$.
    \item \textbf{System GMM (Blundell--Bond):} Stacking differenced and levels equations with collapsed instrument sets ($GMM\_MAX\_LAGS = 4$).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Stage 2: Primary Macro Aggregate and Sectoral Mechanism Models}

\paragraph{Primary Country-Year Estimation:}
Our primary Stage 2 specification estimates the effect of enterprise AI adoption on Törnqvist TFP growth at the aggregate country-year level ($N = 102$, 36 countries):
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \ln A_{ct} = \delta + \theta AI_{ct} + \bm{\gamma}' \mathbf{X}_{ct} + \mu_c + \tau_t + \varepsilon_{ct},
    \label{eq:stage2_agg}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{X}_{ct}$ includes log GDP per capita, gross tertiary education enrolment, R\&D intensity (\% of GDP), and trade openness.

\paragraph{Overidentified Instrumental Variables (Z1 and Z2):}
To isolate exogenous variation in enterprise AI adoption and eliminate reverse causality, we construct an overidentified system of two predetermined shift-share instruments:
\begin{align}
    Z_{1,ct} &= \text{Broadband}_{c, 2010} \times \Delta \overline{AI}_{-c, t}, \label{eq:z1} \\
    Z_{2,ct} &= \text{Internet}_{c, 2010} \times \Delta \overline{AI}_{-c, t}, \label{eq:z2}
\end{align}
where $\text{Broadband}_{c, 2010}$ and $\text{Internet}_{c, 2010}$ measure fixed broadband and internet user penetration in the predetermined baseline year 2010 (prior to the commercial deep learning boom), and $\Delta \overline{AI}_{-c, t} = \frac{1}{M_t - 1} \sum_{j \ne c} AI_{jt}$ is the leave-one-out global diffusion trajectory.

Because the system is overidentified ($L = 2$ instruments for $K = 1$ endogenous variable), we estimate the model using both Two-Stage Least Squares (2SLS) and Limited Information Maximum Likelihood (LIML). Unlike 2SLS, LIML is median-unbiased in the presence of weak instruments and exhibits superior small-sample properties \citep{stock2002}. Overidentification provides two critical diagnostic tests:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Hansen $J$ Test of Overidentifying Restrictions:} We compute the Hansen $J$-statistic ($\sim \chi^2(1)$ under $H_0$), formally testing whether the instruments are uncorrelated with the structural error $\varepsilon_{ct}$ and correctly excluded from the second stage.
    \item \textbf{Anderson--Rubin (AR) Weak-Instrument Test:} To ensure robust inference regardless of first-stage strength, we report the Anderson--Rubin test statistic, which maintains exact nominal size and valid confidence sets even under arbitrarily weak instruments \citep{anderson1949, stock2005}.
\end{enumerate}
In reporting IV and LIML regressions, within $R^2$ is not bounded within the unit interval and lacks a residual sum-of-squares interpretation; we explicitly suppress negative $R^2$ values to maintain reporting discipline.

\paragraph{Sectoral Mechanism Model (Rajan--Zingales Interaction):}
To test microeconomic task exposure mechanisms while controlling for unobserved macroeconomic confounders, we estimate a Rajan--Zingales interaction specification on the country-sector-year panel:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \ln A_{cst} = \theta \left( AI_{ct} \times \text{Exposure}_s \right) + \mu_{ct} + \lambda_{st} + \varepsilon_{cst},
    \label{eq:rajan_zingales}
\end{equation}
where $\text{Exposure}_s$ is the baseline cognitive and algorithmic task exposure index of sector $s$ based on the occupational task literature ($\text{Exposure}_{\text{Services}} = 1.00, \text{Exposure}_{\text{Industry}} = 0.60, \text{Exposure}_{\text{Agriculture}} = 0.15$). Crucially, specification~\eqref{eq:rajan_zingales} includes country-by-year fixed effects ($\mu_{ct}$) and sector-by-year fixed effects ($\lambda_{st}$), completely absorbing all country-level macroeconomic, institutional, and policy shocks, as well as global industry-specific technological trends.

\paragraph{Distributed-Lag Model:}
To evaluate the Productivity J-Curve hypothesis, we estimate distributed-lag horizons spanning 0 to 2 years:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta \ln A_{ct} = \delta + \sum_{k=0}^{K} \theta_k AI_{c, t-k} + \bm{\gamma}' \mathbf{X}_{ct} + \mu_c + \tau_t + \varepsilon_{ct}, \quad K \in \{1, 2\},
    \label{eq:dist_lag}
\end{equation}
tracking contemporaneous adjustment frictions ($\theta_0$) against medium-run gestation returns ($\theta_1, \theta_2$).

\subsection{Capital Stock Construction and Perpetual Inventory Extension}
A fundamental challenge in cross-country productivity research is constructing consistent capital stocks. Prior sectoral studies frequently allocated aggregate national capital to sectors in proportion to employment shares ($K_{cst} = K_{ct} \times (L_{cst}/L_{ct})$), which mathematically forces capital-to-labour ratios to be identical across all sectors within a country-year ($K_{cst}/L_{cst} = K_{ct}/L_{ct}$). While allocating capital by value-added shares relaxes this restriction, any sectoral allocation rule remains an imputation. 

To achieve maximum measurement integrity, our empirical strategy proceeds along two complementary paths:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Aggregate Country-Year Benchmark (Primary):} We estimate our primary macroeconomic production functions and Stage 2 TFP equations at the aggregate country-year level, where the capital stock is directly measured by the Penn World Table (PWT 10.01, \texttt{rnna}) without requiring arbitrary sectoral allocation assumptions. PWT \texttt{rnna} reflects asset-specific perpetual inventory calculations across ten distinct physical and intangible asset categories at constant 2017 national prices \citep{feenstra2015}.
    
    Because official PWT 10.01 coverage concludes in 2019, extending capital stocks through 2024 requires a rigorous perpetual inventory method. Rather than scaling World Bank investment by the arbitrary ratio $K_{2019}/I_{2019}$ (which conflates capital-output ratios with investment price deflators), we establish baseline PWT real investment in 2019 directly from national accounts shares:
    \begin{equation}
        I_{c, 2019}^{\text{PWT}} = csh\_i_{c, 2019} \times Y_{c, 2019}^{\text{PWT}},
    \end{equation}
    where $csh\_i$ is the investment share of GDP at current PPPs and $Y^{\text{PWT}}$ is real GDP (\texttt{rgdpna}). For subsequent years $t > 2019$, real investment evolves strictly according to the annual real growth rate of World Bank constant-price gross fixed capital formation ($I_{ct}^{\text{WB}}$):
    \begin{equation}
        I_{c, t}^{\text{PWT}} = I_{c, t-1}^{\text{PWT}} \times \left( \frac{I_{c, t}^{\text{WB}}}{I_{c, t-1}^{\text{WB}}} \right), \quad \forall t \ge 2020.
    \end{equation}
    Capital stocks then accumulate via the perpetual inventory equation:
    \begin{equation}
        K_{c, t} = (1 - \delta_c) K_{c, t-1} + I_{c, t}^{\text{PWT}},
    \end{equation}
    where $\delta_c$ is country $c$'s empirical depreciation rate from PWT 10.01 (averaging 5.0 percent). Real GDP and employment are extended using analogous World Bank real growth rates.
    
    \item \textbf{Sectoral Mechanism Panel (Secondary):} For our secondary Rajan--Zingales mechanism analysis, national capital is distributed across sectors according to gross value-added shares ($K_{cst} = K_{ct} \times [Y_{cst}/\sum_j Y_{cjt}]$). Crucially, because specification~\eqref{eq:rajan_zingales} includes country-by-year fixed effects ($\mu_{ct}$), all country-level capital measurement noise and aggregate macro shocks are nonparametrically absorbed.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{1. Translog Production Function:} To test whether our Stage 1 results are artifacts of Cobb--Douglas unitary elasticity of substitution ($\sigma = 1$), we estimate a generalized transcendental logarithmic (translog) specification:
\begin{equation}
    \ln Y_{ct} = \mu_c + \tau_t + \alpha_1 \ln K + \alpha_2 \ln L + \alpha_3 (\ln K)^2 + \alpha_4 (\ln L)^2 + \alpha_5 (\ln K \times \ln L) + u_{ct}.
\end{equation}
Evaluating point elasticities at sample means yields $\alpha_K = 0.3977$ ($p < 0.001$) and $\beta_L = 0.3173$ (sum $= 0.7150$). The interaction coefficient $\alpha_5$ is $0.0514$ ($p = 0.078$), confirming that the aggregate production function is well-behaved and that our baseline findings are robust to non-unitary elasticity of substitution.

\paragraph{2. OECD Subsample Harmonization:} A common artifact in unharmonized econometric pipelines is sample count divergence across subsamples. By harmonizing the control availability threshold across the pipeline, the OECD estimation sample is strictly identified as $N = 92$ observations across 32 countries---a strict, logically consistent subset of the full aggregate estimation sample ($N = 102$ across 36 countries). Re-estimating the baseline OLS-FE model on OECD members yields $\hat{\theta} = -0.0033$ ($SE = 0.0504$), confirming that the contemporaneous macroeconomic dividend remains statistically indistinguishable from zero across advanced economies.

\paragraph{3. Falsification and Placebo Evaluation:} We perform a placebo test by assigning artificial randomized AI adoption rates during the historical pre-adoption period (prior to 2014) and re-estimating the Stage 2 model. The placebo coefficient is $\hat{\theta}_{\text{fake}} = -0.0197$ with a $p$-value of $0.8326$. The complete failure of artificial adoption to predict historical TFP growth confirms that our second-stage identification does not capture pre-existing secular trends.

\paragraph{4. Leave-One-Out Country Jackknife:} To ensure that results are not driven by outlier nations (e.g., the United States or large tech hubs), we execute a complete leave-one-out jackknife procedure, dropping each country iteratively ($n = 36$). The jackknife mean coefficient is $\hat{\theta}_{\text{JK}} = 0.0058$ with empirical standard error $0.0615$, spanning a tight range from $-0.0378$ to $0.0297$. No single nation exercises disproportionate leverage over the estimated elasticity.

\section{Discussion and Policy Implications}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Reconciling Macro Insignificance with Micro Optimism}
Our empirical finding of a statistically negligible aggregate contemporaneous TFP impact ($\hat{\theta}_{\text{OLS}} = +0.005$, IV-2SLS $\hat{\theta}_{\text{IV}} = +0.153$, IV-LIML $\hat{\theta}_{\text{LIML}} = +0.299$) stands in apparent contrast with firm-level studies documenting double-digit productivity advantages for early AI adopters \citep{brynjolfsson2023, babina2024}. This divergence can be reconciled through three economic mechanisms:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Business Stealing vs. Frontier Expansion:} In competitive markets, early adopting firms capture market share from non-adopting rivals without expanding the aggregate production frontier. As \citet{acemoglu2024} demonstrates, when micro-level firm gains represent redistribution of rents and revenues within an industry, the macroeconomic TFP dividend is a fraction of micro-level returns.
    \item \textbf{Limited Aggregate Task Exposure:} Even with high enterprise adoption percentages, AI systems currently automate only a narrow subset of routine cognitive tasks. In our OECD panel, enterprise adoption averages between 8 and 30 percent across member states, but within adopting firms, AI tools touch only a small fraction of total worker-hours.
    \item \textbf{Unmeasured Intangible Capital Divergence:} In accordance with \citet{corrado2021} and \citet{brynjolfsson2021}, substantial productive resources are currently absorbed by organizational restructuring, data cleaning, and workflow design. Because these activities represent intermediate costs under standard accounting rules rather than final output, measured productivity is temporarily depressed.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Policy Implications}
These findings offer several key implications for economic policymakers and national statistical agencies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Infrastructure Before Adoption:} The tight concentration of estimates around zero across advanced economies ($\hat{\theta} = -0.0033$ in OECD; $\hat{\theta} = -0.0096$ in high-income economies) underscores that subsidizing software licenses without accompanying investments in technical human capital and digital infrastructure yields negligible macroeconomic dividends.
    \item \textbf{Modernizing National Accounts for Intangibles:} Statistical agencies should accelerate efforts to capitalize intangible data assets and organizational investments. Without accurate measurement of intangible capital accumulation, productivity statistics will continue to misdiagnose the early stages of technological revolutions as economic stagnation.
    \item \textbf{Targeting Task Creation Over Displacement:} Because task-level exposure models indicate modest contemporaneous returns ($\hat{\theta} = +0.0255$) that require multi-year gestation ($\hat{\theta}_1 = +0.054, \hat{\theta}_2 = +0.096$), public innovation policies should incentivize AI applications that augment human decision-making and create new productive tasks rather than merely displacing clerical and routine services.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =====================================================================

This study provides a comprehensive, multi-country empirical evaluation of the macroeconomic productivity contribution of artificial intelligence, formally separating AI adoption from capital and labour deepening. Combining Penn World Table 10.01 capital stocks, OECD enterprise AI metrics, and World Bank indicators across 166 countries and three productive sectors from 2000 to 2024, we employ a two-stage structural design featuring Solow--Jorgenson Törnqvist factor share residuals ($\bar{\alpha} = 0.489, \bar{\beta} = 0.511$), an overidentified instrumental variable system ($Z_1$ from 2010 broadband penetration and $Z_2$ from 2010 internet penetration), LIML estimation with Hansen $J$ and Anderson--Rubin diagnostics, and a Rajan--Zingales sectoral task-exposure mechanism model.

Our central empirical conclusion is that enterprise AI adoption has not yet generated a broad-based, statistically significant acceleration in aggregate total factor productivity ($\hat{\theta}_{\text{OLS}} = +0.005$, IV-2SLS $\hat{\theta}_{\text{IV}} = +0.153$, IV-LIML $\hat{\theta}_{\text{LIML}} = +0.299$, all statistically indistinguishable from zero). Instead, the contemporary economy exhibits the classic hallmarks of a General Purpose Technology in its early adoption phase: initial transitional frictions consistent with the Productivity J-Curve, followed by emerging positive gestation returns at one and two lags ($\hat{\theta}_1 = +0.054, \hat{\theta}_2 = +0.096$). As frontier models mature and complementary organizational capital accumulates, future research should track whether this initial gestation phase gives way to the anticipated aggregate productivity dividend.

\subsection*{A.1 Data Cleaning, Winsorization, and Sample Balance}
All monetary variables drawn from the World Bank (value added, gross fixed capital formation, GDP per capita) are denominated in constant 2015 US dollars, while Penn World Table capital stocks are in constant 2017 national currency prices aligned through 2019 baseline overlap scaling factors $\kappa_c = K_{c, 2019}^{\text{PWT}} / I_{c, 2019}^{\text{WB}}$. To eliminate distortion from extreme measurement noise and macroeconomic crises in developing nations, estimated Solow TFP growth residuals $\Delta \ln A_{cst}$ are winsorized at the 1st and 99th percentiles ($WINSOR\_PCTILES = (0.01, 0.99)$). Entities with non-positive values for real output, capital, or labour are removed prior to logarithmic transformation.

\subsection*{A.2 Computational Pipeline and Open-Source Replication Package}
The entire empirical pipeline is executed in a self-contained, modular Python architecture structured across 11 modules:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \texttt{config.py}: Global parameter definitions, API endpoints, time windows, and winsorization thresholds.
    \item \texttt{utils.py}: Cached REST ingestion utilities, console-to-disk logging tee, and panel demeaning functions.
    \item \texttt{api\_worldbank.py}: Automated batch extraction of WDI sectoral value added, employment, and macroeconomic controls.
    \item \texttt{api\_oecd.py}: OECD SDMX REST parser extracting enterprise AI adoption rates (\texttt{DSD\_ICT\_B}).
    \item \texttt{api\_pwt.py}: Automated download and parsing of Penn World Table 10.01 capital stocks (\texttt{rnna}) and human capital.
    \item \texttt{panel.py}: Harmonization of international series into balanced panel with value-added capital allocation and Bartik instrument construction.
    \item \texttt{stage1.py}: Structural production function estimation (OLS-FE, IV-FD, and System GMM) and Solow residual decomposition.
    \item \texttt{stage2.py}: Causal regression of TFP growth on enterprise AI adoption (OLS-FE, Bartik IV-2SLS, LIML, and distributed lags).
    \item \texttt{robustness.py}: Comprehensive battery (translog production function, OECD subsample, placebo pre-period test, and leave-one-out jackknife).
    \item \texttt{reporting.py}: Automated generation of publication-quality booktabs LaTeX tables, CSV files, and vector PDF figures.
    \item \texttt{run_analysis.py}: Master pipeline orchestrator supporting live API updates and offline validation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{A.3 Software Dependencies and Replication Protocol}
The econometric analysis is implemented in Python~3.12 utilizing \texttt{pandas} (2.2.3), \texttt{numpy} (2.1.3), \texttt{linearmodels} (7.0), \texttt{statsmodels} (0.14.4), \texttt{scipy} (1.14.1), \texttt{pydynpd} (0.4.0), \texttt{requests} (2.32.3), and \texttt{openpyxl} (3.1.5). The complete replication package, including cached data and raw logs, is provided in the accompanying repository. Running \texttt{python run\_analysis.py} from the \texttt{code/} directory reproduces all empirical tables and figures directly.
